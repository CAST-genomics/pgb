Single-hue ramp candidates

HeatmapLook colors nodes with blues, PGB’s only single-hue ramp. This page draws four published alternatives beside it, as the 3D graph would draw them, on the heatmap scene’s background.

This page is the visual companion to single-hue-sequential-ramps-research.md, which surveys 35 ramps and cites a source for each claim. It assumes frequency-color-ramps.html, which explains the lightness plots and the “clearly visible” threshold.

The candidates

Each candidate runs from light at 0% to dark at 100%, so a darker node means a population carries it more often. Each is close enough to one hue that lightness alone orders the nodes.

Crameri, cmocean and Kovesi publish 256-entry tables. Nine evenly spaced samples, blended PGB’s way, reproduce each full table to within ΔE2000 1.2, which is about the smallest difference anyone notices.

Crameri devon stays in the blue family but leans toward violet, and ends at a dark indigo. cmocean algae is a true single green, and its 0% end is already a pale green rather than white. cmocean amp is a single red from warm off-white to maroon. CET-L12 is the purest single blue, and it stops at a mid blue rather than going dark.

How evenly each one darkens

A ramp is perceptually even when equal steps in frequency look like equal steps in color, which for a single hue means lightness falls along a straight line. Figure 2 plots each ramp’s OKLab lightness against that line.

The three published perceptual ramps, algae, amp and CET-L12, sit almost on their dashed lines. devon wanders a little. blues bows above its line through the lower half, staying pale until about 40%: it was designed as nine separate classes, not a continuous scale, and its lightness steps were hand-tuned.

The ends differ too. devon, algae and amp run to about 0.24–0.27, darker than blues at 0.32. CET-L12 stops at 0.57. That gives it about half the range, and so fewer steps a viewer can tell apart, but no node ever goes dark.

Liu & Heer (CHI 2018) found that dark shades on a white background are hard to tell apart, and advised against such colormaps on light backgrounds. Their test used 50 px squares, not thin ribbons.

algae is the only candidate whose 0% end is already visible. Rare nodes stay faintly green, so it needs no 0.25 + 0.75 * frequency offset. amp becomes visible almost as quickly. devon and CET-L12 start from near-white like blues, but cross the threshold sooner because they darken evenly from the start.

Choosing between them

Colorblindness does not separate them. All five keep their lightness in order under simulated deuteranopia, protanopia and tritanopia (Machado 2009 model), so all five still order the nodes for colorblind viewers. What separates them is hue, evenness, and how they meet the background.

RampChoose it ifWhat it costs
Crameri devonYou want to stay blue, but with lightness that falls about twice as evenly as blues.A violet lean, and a near-white 0% end, so rare nodes still fade.
cmocean algaeYou want rare nodes to stay visible without an offset, and a hue nothing else in PGB uses.The dark end, #122414, may read as black on thin ribbons. Under red–green colorblindness it loses its green, though not its order.
cmocean ampYou want a warm hue.Red reads as “hot” or “alarm”. PGB’s rubinClay emphasis color is also warm.
CET-L12You want a quiet heatmap that never goes dark, or ribbons may one day be lit.About half the lightness range, so fewer distinguishable steps.
bluesFamiliarity matters most.The pale lower half shown in Figure 2.

Any single-hue ramp gives up some resolution: Liu & Heer found single-hue ramps less accurate than multi-hue ones like viridis for comparing nearby values. That is the price of reading frequency from one color.

What to avoid

Gray ramps are the natural next thought for a single hue, and they do not work in PGB. Edges, and every node before a population is selected, are rubinGray. Midway along any gray ramp the two nearly coincide.

Also avoid CET-L13 to L15, which run from black and were built for three-color composite images, and the multi-hue maps in the research note’s table, such as lapaz, tempo and ice.

Stops for color-ramps.js

Each block drops into src/utils/color/color-ramps.js beside blues, and is already ordered light to dark. Swap the call in HeatmapLook.handleSelectionEvent to try one.

// Crameri v8.0.1, devon10, reversed, light to dark
export const devon = _makeRamp([
  '#ffffff', '#e8e5fa', '#d0ccf5', '#bab3f1', '#989be7',
  '#6181d0', '#3669ad', '#275186', '#293467', '#2c1a4c',
]);
// cmocean algae-rgb.txt, 9 samples, light to dark
export const algae = _makeRamp([
  '#d7f9d0', '#afdda2', '#84c47a', '#50ad59', '#129450',
  '#0c7849', '#185b3b', '#194029', '#122414',
]);
// cmocean amp-rgb.txt, 9 samples, light to dark
export const amp = _makeRamp([
  '#f1edec', '#e2c7bf', '#d7a291', '#cc7e64', '#c0583b',
  '#af3024', '#901029', '#650f24', '#3c0912',
]);
// Kovesi CET-L12.csv, 9 samples, light to dark
export const cetL12 = _makeRamp([
  '#f0f1f0', '#d8e1ee', '#c0d2eb', '#a8c4e6', '#93b5dc',
  '#82a6cf', '#7197c1', '#5a89b8', '#3b7bb2',

Licences: Crameri and cmocean are MIT; CET is CC BY 4.0 and asks that Kovesi (2015) be cited; ColorBrewer is Apache 2.0. Take Crameri stops from the Zenodo v8.0.1 archive: the cmcrameri GitHub copies predate v8 for some maps.

The devon stops are Crameri’s own ten-color palette, reversed. The others are nine samples taken from the publishers’ 256-entry tables for this page.

Two comments in color-ramps.js are worth fixing while you are there. The comment on blues calls it print-safe, but ColorBrewer rates nine-class Blues as neither print- nor LCD-friendly; it does rate it colorblind-friendly. The header says ramps have 8 stops; blues has 9.
